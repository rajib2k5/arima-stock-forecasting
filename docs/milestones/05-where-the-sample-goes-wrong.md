The sections below take them in that order. "The course" is the sample course outline the project
follows (credited in the README); what it does is described in our words, from the module structure
that ADR-0002 and the milestone 3 and 4 walkthroughs already record.

### 1. The order chosen on the whole series

**What the course does.** It runs pmdarima's stepwise `auto_arima` on the log close of the whole
course window, 2015 to 2023, and only then splits off the last tenth as the test set. It keeps the
order the search chose for the model it fits on the training rows.

**Why it is wrong.** The test set's job is to be data the model has never influenced. Here the 227 test
rows take part in choosing the order, so the test score belongs to a procedure that saw the test. On
another ticker or another period, those rows could change the order, and the test would then favour
the order that fits the test.

**What it costs on AAPL.** Nothing measurable. The search on all 2,264 rows chose ARIMA(0, 1, 1) with an
intercept; the same search on the 2,037 training rows chose the same (`same_order`: true). For a model
with no AR term, pmdarima's intercept is the daily drift itself: 0.0009097 on the full series, against
0.0008993 for the same order on the training rows. That the leak changed nothing here is a fact about
this series, not a property of the procedure.

**Where it is guarded.** Order selection runs on the training rows only
(`tests/test_export.py` `test_the_order_selection_payloads_use_the_training_rows_only`), and
`course_track` (`src/arima_forecasting/fitting.py:245`) runs the course's search on the full series
only to measure the leak, beside the training-only run of milestone 2.

### 2. The static path as "the forecast"

**What the course does.** It fits the model on the training rows, calls `get_forecast` once for as
many steps as there are test days, exponentiates the path, and plots it over the test prices as the
forecast.

**Why it is wrong.** That path is one forecast made on one evening: its first day is one step ahead
and its last day is 227 steps ahead. For an ARIMA(0, 1, 1) without a drift, every step after the first
has the same forecast (concept note 10 derives it), so the path is a flat line. It is the model's honest
answer to "what will the price be in h days?", and it says almost nothing: the plot invites a reader
to judge a 227-day forecast as if it were 227 daily ones. And with no baseline under it, a flat line
cannot be told from a skilful one.

**What it costs on AAPL.** The course's median path is flat at $151.47 for all 227 test days (the
origin's close was $151.79; the first step moves it by −0.00213 log points, and every later step adds
exactly 0). The price ranged from $142.98 to $195.72. Its RMSE, $25.991, is 1.07% above that of a flat
line at the origin's price ($25.717). On the out-of-sample window the path is flat at $190.29 while the
price ranged over $177.85; its ratio is 0.9991. The one-step forecasts of the same model, made each
evening from that day's data, have an RMSE of $2.102 on the test window, against the random walk's
$2.087 (ratio 1.0075).

With a drift, the static path is no longer flat. The AIC model's path rises from $151.43 to $185.64
over the test window, and its RMSE is 0.4134 of the flat line's. A random walk that carries the AIC model's drift
has an RMSE of $10.339 against the AIC model's $10.632 on the same test path (milestone 4's probe, measured
2026-10-29). That is one path each, not a test; it points to the drift in a rising market rather than
the ARMA terms.

**Where it is guarded.** Every model is forecast both ways (`src/arima_forecasting/forecasting.py:134`
static, `:142` walk-forward), each beside its own random walk, and the app labels the static path as
the course's.

### 3. A dollar error with no baseline

**What the course does.** It reports the RMSE, MAE and MAPE of its forecast in dollars, and stops.

**Why it is wrong.** A score means nothing alone. Whether $26 is good depends on what a forecast that
knows nothing would score on the same days. For a stock price that is the random walk: tomorrow's
price is today's (one step), or every day's price is the origin's (static).

**What it costs on AAPL.** The course's static test numbers, RMSE $25.991, MAE $22.849 and MAPE
12.676%, look like a result. The flat random walk scores $25.717, $22.597 and 12.537%: the course's
forecast is worse on all three. One step ahead, every model on both windows has a dollar RMSE 1.0056
to 1.0174 times the random walk's (log RMSE 1.0054 to 1.0175); none is below 1. The DM-HLN tests
(per test, uncorrected) say "worse than the random walk" in 5 of the 16 tests, all on the out-of-sample
window (aic walk-forward absolute, p 0.0100; aic refit absolute, p 0.0103; bic walk-forward squared,
p 0.0480, and absolute, p 0.0002; course walk-forward absolute, p 0.0242), "no difference" in the
other 11, and "better" in none. None of this can be seen without the baseline.

**Where it is guarded.** The random walk sits under every metric in `evaluation.json` (`random_walk`,
`ratio_to_rw`), in the app and in the README's table.

### 4. exp of a log forecast as the expected price

**What the course does.** It forecasts the log price and exponentiates the forecast mean, and treats
the result as the price to expect.

**Why it is wrong.** If the log price is normal with mean μ and variance s², exp(μ) is the price's
median. Its mean is exp(μ + s²/2), higher by the factor exp(s²/2). The two are close when s is small
(one day ahead) and far apart when it is large (a long static path). Which one to report depends on the
loss: the mean minimises the expected squared error, the median the expected absolute error. Calling
the median "the expected price" is a mislabel either way.

**What it costs on AAPL.** One step ahead, the mean is above the median by at most 0.0165% to 0.0177%
per path: nothing. At the end of the course's static test path, the median is $151.47 and the mean
$156.73, 3.4714% (+$5.26) higher; at the end of the out-of-sample path, $190.29 and $211.05, 10.9072%
(+$20.76) higher. Scored by RMSE, the mean does better than the median on 5 of the 6 static paths (the
course's test path: $23.176 against $25.991), and worse on the AIC model's out-of-sample path ($49.817
against $34.256), whose median path ran above the price on 603 of its 689 days.

**Where it is guarded.** Every forecast row carries both (`median_usd`, `mean_usd`), every metric is
scored on both, and `back_transform` states the gap. The README's table uses the median, as the
course does, and says so.

### 5. The dropped drift

**What the course does.** After the search, it fits `ARIMA(train, order=order)` with every other
argument left at statsmodels' default. It carries over the order, not the intercept the search chose.

**Why it is wrong.** In statsmodels 0.15, `ARIMA` has no trend term by default when d ≥ 1
(`src/arima_forecasting/fitting.py:215` cites the line). The search chose ARIMA(0, 1, 1) with an
intercept, which at d = 1 is a drift; the refit silently fits a different model, without one. Nothing
warns.

**What it costs on AAPL.** The most of any mistake. On the training rows the drift is 0.0008993 log
points a day (OPG standard error 0.0003885, p 0.0206), and the model with it has an AIC 3.546 lower
(`drift_dropped`). Put back, it changes the static paths completely: the course's order with its drift
has a static test RMSE of $10.547 (ratio to the flat line 0.4101) instead of $25.991, and an
out-of-sample RMSE of $34.175 (0.5357) instead of $63.738. Its last-day median is $185.77 instead of
$151.47 on the test window (actual $190.21), and $353.63 instead of $190.29 out of sample (actual
$333.02). One step ahead the drift adds about 0.09% to a day's forecast, and the RMSE barely moves:
$2.096 with it, $2.102 without, $2.087 for the random walk (test); $4.074, $4.078 and $4.044 (oos).

The drift paid off because the price kept rising at about the training rate: the realised mean daily
log return was 1.11 times the training drift on the test window and 0.90 times it out of sample. A
random walk with the same drift scores $10.364 on the same test path: one path each, not a test, and
again it points to the drift rather than the ARMA terms.
Had the market turned, the same drift would have cost.

**Where it is guarded.** The grid fits every order with and without a drift and chooses the trend
with the order (ADR-0002); `course_track` refits the course's order with the drift to measure the
gap; `tests/test_fitting.py:173` pins that the course's refit has trend `"n"`.

### 6. The burned first residual

**What the course does.** It plots the residuals of its refit (a line, a density and a QQ plot) as
`resid` returns them, the first one included.

**Why it is wrong.** With d = 1 the model's starting level is unknown. statsmodels starts it with an
approximately diffuse prior: the forecast of the first day is 0 with a variance of about 10⁶. So the
first residual is the first log price itself, a level, not a shock. statsmodels' own diagnostic plots
drop it, by the rule max(`loglikelihood_burn`, `nobs_diffuse`) (`src/arima_forecasting/diagnostics.py:63`
cites the line).

**What it costs on AAPL.** The course's first residual is 3.18518, the log close of 2015-01-02. The
largest of the other 2,036 in size is 0.1295, so the first is 24.6 times larger (30.4 times the next
highest point, 0.1048, at the top of a QQ plot). The other 2,036 span 7% of the residuals' range. The
moments change by orders of magnitude: Pearson kurtosis 1,774.27 with it and 7.76 without, skewness
40.683 and −0.261, standard deviation 0.07299 and 0.01879, Jarque–Bera 266,847,641.9 and 1,948.6. Its
standardised value is 0.0032, so a plot of standardised residuals (as statsmodels draws them) would hide
it; a plot of `resid` shows it as the biggest feature on the page.

**Where it is guarded.** Every test and plot uses the standardised residuals after the burn-in
(`tests/test_diagnostics.py:121`); the burned row is exported (`burned`) and shown on the Fit page, so a
reader sees what the course plots.

### 7. Module 9: the forecast call

**What the course does.** It builds the model on a DataFrame indexed by trading dates and asks
`get_forecast` for as many steps as there are test days.

**Why it is wrong.** Trading dates have no fixed frequency (holidays), so statsmodels cannot extend the
index into the future. In statsmodels 0.15 the call raises a `ValueError` whose message starts "No
supported index is available".

**What it costs.** The course's module 9 makes no forecast on these library versions.
`tests/test_forecasting.py:288` reproduces it on a simulated series with trading-day dates.

**Where it is guarded.** Every model here is built on a numpy array, and the dates are put back from
the price table (`src/arima_forecasting/forecasting.py:106`); the same test shows ours forecasts the
same dates.

### 8. Module 10: the MAPE

**What the course does.** It computes the MAPE from the exponentiated forecast (a pandas Series) and the
exponentiated test prices.

**Why it is wrong.** yfinance 1.7.0 returns the close as a one-column DataFrame. A Series minus a
DataFrame lines the Series' index up against the DataFrame's column labels, not its rows, so every
cell of the result is NaN, and so is their mean.

**What it costs.** The course's MAPE prints `nan`. `tests/test_evaluation.py:52` reproduces it on three
made-up prices.

**Where it is guarded.** The metrics take one-dimensional numbers only and refuse a frame
(`src/arima_forecasting/evaluation.py:86`); the same test shows the hand value from the frame's column.

## Pitfalls met and how each is guarded

| Pitfall | What happened | Guard |
|---|---|---|
| **"30 times the next largest"** | The README's first draft said the burned residual was 30 times the next largest. That is the next highest point (0.1048); the largest of the others in size is −0.1295, and the ratio to it is 24.6 | The probe prints both; the README and this walkthrough say which is which |
| **Which drift a drifted random walk carries** | The AIC model's drift (0.000901858, milestone 4) and the course's training drift (0.0008993) give $10.339 and $10.364 on the test window | Each figure names its drift |
